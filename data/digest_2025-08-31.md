# **Lewis and Clark’s "Thunder-Clappers": How Mercury-Laced Laxatives Helped Archaeologists Trace Their Historic Journey**  

### **"Blazing a Trail with Pills and Pits"**  

When Lewis and Clark embarked on their legendary Corps of Expedition in the early 1800s, they weren’t just mapping uncharted territory—they were also dealing with an all-too-familiar frontier struggle: **constant constipation**.  

Their low-fiber diet left the entire crew painfully backed up, but they came prepared—with **600 "thunder-clappers,"** hefty mercury-laced laxatives designed to keep things *moving*. Little did they know, these “Bilious Pills” would one day act as tiny historical breadcrumbs for archaeologists.

### **How a Frontier Constipation Cure Became a Scientific Goldmine**  

The active ingredient in the pills—**mercury**—doesn’t break down easily in soil. Centuries later, researchers searching for Lewis and Clark’s campsites simply look for **mercury-polluted latrine pits**. By matching these pits with military records of their encampments, historians can pinpoint their exact trail stops—like a **bio-archaeological treasure hunt**.

### **The Dubious Hero of Early American Medicine**  

The infamous "thunder-clappers" were the brainchild of **Dr. Benjamin Rush**, a Founding Father and Thomas Jefferson’s pick as the nation’s top doctor. Despite his prestigious reputation, Rush subscribed to **“heroic medicine”**—a brutal approach that included **bloodletting, blistering, and massive purgative doses** under the belief that disease stemmed from bodily fluid imbalances (a theory dating back to **Roman physician Galen**).  

As history later proved, Rush's methods often **harmed more than healed**, but his crude mercury-packed pills left behind **a bizarre but invaluable archaeological marker**—ensuring that the explorers' digestive struggles would be remembered forever.

### **The Irony of Frontier Medicine**  

Ironically, **the absence of a trained physician** on the journey may have saved lives. As historian David Peck notes, a doctor might have **bled patients to death** or overdosed them on mercury. Instead, Lewis and Clark relied on trial, error, and those **notorious laxatives**—proving that sometimes, **bad medicine makes for great history.**

# The Hidden Security Measure Behind WWII Telegram Warnings  

During World War II, sensitive telegrams often bore a cryptic instruction: *"This telegram must be closely paraphrased before being communicated to anyone."* At first glance, this seems counterintuitive—if secrecy was crucial, why not just keep the original message intact? The answer lies in wartime cryptography and a clever tactic to protect encoding systems.  

## Why Paraphrase Messages?  

A declassified 1950 **U.S. Army cryptography manual** (*TM 32-220*) reveals the reason: repeating the exact same message—whether encrypted or in plaintext—posed a major security risk. If an enemy intercepted both versions, they could compare them to **crack the encryption**.  

### The Rules of Text Safety:  
- **Never repeat identical messages** in both encrypted and plain forms.  
- If information had to be shared widely (e.g., in press releases), **rewriting it drastically** before distribution was required.  

### How to Paraphrase Properly:  
1. **Reorganize sentences** (change word order, clause positions).  
2. **Swap synonyms** (replace repeated names with pronouns like *"the former"*).  
3. **Condense instead of expanding**—adding fluff could let cryptanalysts reconstruct the original.  

## A Simple Security Fix  
This rule wasn’t just wartime pedantry; it was a **key defense** against codebreakers. In an era where encryption was frequently manual (e.g., Enigma machines), preventing plaintext leaks was critical.  

### Modern Echoes  
Even today, cybersecurity practices avoid **data repetition**—password reuse is a well-known vulnerability. Turns out, good op-sec hasn’t changed much in 80 years.
