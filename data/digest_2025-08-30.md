### **Beyond the Checklist: Why Great Work Demands Emotion**  

**By Mitchell Hashimoto**  

There’s a quiet, often overlooked truth in technology and product design—**success isn’t just about ticking boxes**. You can ship features on time, meet KPIs, and deliver a flawless demo, yet still miss the most critical part: **how it *feels***.  

### **The Feels Matter**  
Every interaction—whether with a website, app, or physical object—evokes an emotional response: joy, frustration, surprise, relief. Great products don’t just *function*; they create a visceral connection.  

You know it when you see it:  
- That **subtle delight** when a UI interaction feels effortless.  
- That **"Aha!" moment** when a feature surprises you in the best way.  
- That **instinctive trust** when everything just *works*.  

### **You Can’t Fake It**  
Metrics and specs alone won’t capture this. **You have to *live* in the work.**  
- Use it like a real user.  
- Play with it when no one's watching.  
- Notice the moments that make you **smile or grimace**.  

### **The Takeaway**  
➡ **Feeling is feedback.**  
➡ **Joy is a feature, not a bug.**  
➡ **If it doesn’t resonate with *you*, why would it resonate with anyone else?**  

Next time you build, don’t just check the boxes—**feel it.**

### Why did books start being divided into chapters? A new history

#### [Submission URL](https://sydneyreviewofbooks.com/reviews/just-a-little-longer) | 128 points | by [cacher](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=cacher) | [50 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45077735)

# **Why Books Have Chapters: A Literary History of Time Itself**  

Ever picked up an old book and felt lost in its unfamiliar rhythm? Lydia Davis once tried to "translate" a 1768 novel into modern English—not because the language was foreign, but because its structure was alien. Laurence Sterne’s *A Sentimental Journey* begins mid-conversation, with erratic dashes and bizarrely repeating chapter titles ("The Monk | Calais" appears *three times* in just a few pages).  

Sterne’s chaotic chaptering throws a crucial question into sharp relief: **Why do books even have chapters?** And what do these divisions reveal about how we experience time?  

That’s the ambitious thread Nicholas Dames follows in *The Chapter: A Segmented History from Antiquity to the Twenty-First Century*. A literary scholar with a deep fascination for how writing shapes our perception of time, Dames explores chapters not just as organizational tools but as "boxes of time" that reflect cultural and cognitive shifts.  

## **From the Bible to the Novel: The Evolution of the Chapter**  
Chapters weren’t always a given. Ancient scrolls had no breaks; early Christian scholars, needing to reference sacred texts efficiently, were among the first to impose divisions. By the Middle Ages, chapters became essential for scholarly navigation. But Dames argues they do more than organize—**they dictate how we feel time pass** as we read.  

In the 18th century, Sterne’s bizarre chaptering mocked convention by stretching and compressing time through absurd repetition. Later, 19th-century novelists used chapters to pace suspense (think Dickens’ cliffhangers). Today, in an era of digital skimming, chapters serve yet another purpose: modular, digestible chunks for distracted readers.  

## **Why This Matters Now**  
Dames’ study doesn’t just excavate old formats—it questions what our *current* chaptering habits say about us. Are we speed-reading through life? Do we need structured pauses, or has fragmentation become our default?  

For book lovers and historians, this is a deep dive into the invisible architecture of reading. For the rest of us? A reminder that **how we break up stories reveals how we break up time itself.**

#### [Submission URL](https://agentclientprotocol.com/overview/introduction) | 267 points | by [vinhnx](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=vinhnx) | [91 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45074147)

### Introducing the Agent Client Protocol (ACP): The Future of AI-Editor Communication  

**Why ACP?**  
AI coding assistants are becoming essential tools for developers, but integrating them with different code editors is a hassle. Editors need custom plugins for each agent, and agents must support multiple editor APIs—leading to fragmented ecosystems and wasted effort. Enter the **Agent Client Protocol (ACP)**, a standardized way for AI agents and code editors to communicate, similar to how the Language Server Protocol (LSP) revolutionized IDE-language server interactions.  

### Key Benefits:  
- **Seamless Integration** – Any ACP-compatible agent works with any ACP-compatible editor.  
- **No More Lock-In** – Developers aren't stuck with limited editor-agent pairings.  
- **Faster Innovation** – Agents and editors evolve independently without reintegration headaches.  

### How It Works  
- **Communication via JSON-RPC** – Agents run as sub-processes of the editor, exchanging structured messages over stdio.  
- **Markdown by Default** – Ensures readability without requiring heavy HTML rendering from the editor.  
- **Supports Advanced Features** – Including diffs, tool calls, and more.  

### Current Ecosystem  
- **Editors Supporting ACP**  
  - [Zed](https://zed.dev/)  
  - Neovim (via the **CodeCompanion** plugin)  
- **AI Agents**  
  - **Gemini** (more coming soon!)  

### Get Involved  
ACP is still evolving, but the foundation is ready for experimentation. Check out the [GitHub repo](https://github.com/zed-industries) and start building!

### Bcachefs Goes to "Externally Maintained"

#### [Submission URL](https://lwn.net/Articles/1035736/) | 194 points | by [ksec](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=ksec) | [304 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45074312)

**Bcachefs Status Shift Sparks Debate on Future Maintenance**  

Linus Torvalds has quietly adjusted the maintainer status of bcachefs (the next-gen Linux filesystem from Kent Overstreet) to "externally maintained," signaling that new changes may not land in the mainline kernel soon—but also that outright removal isn’t imminent.  

### **What Does "Externally Maintained" Mean?**  
Questions swirl over what this label implies for the in-tree code:  
- Will it stagnate, leaving users stuck with an unmaintained version?  
- Or will another maintainer step in to shepherd updates from Overstreet’s out-of-tree work into mainline?  

### **The Bus Factor Problem**  
Commenters note that relying on a single maintainer (Overstreet) is risky, and the move may encourage others to take up maintenance. However, as NYKevin points out, this shifts the burden to a proxy—someone who must mediate between Overstreet’s development pace and the kernel’s strict release cadence.  

### **Overstreet’s Stance**  
Kent himself weighed in, revealing his reluctance to delegate maintenance:  
- He fears burnout for potential successors (especially other bcachefs contributors).  
- He insists on careful release processes, having dealt with fallout from broken distro packaging (like Debian’s bcachefs-tools snafu).  
- No clear path forward yet—even he’s unsure of Linus’s plans.  

### **The Bigger Picture**  
- **Debian Parallels:** Unlike XScreenSaver’s feud with Debian, the kernel team won’t tolerate unmaintained code indefinitely.  
- **Licensing Advantage:** Unlike ZFS, bcachefs is GPL-friendly, so politics-free technical fixes could revive its mainline future.  

For now, the filesystem remains in limbo—neither evolving nor disappearing. The community awaits either a new maintainer stepping up or a clearer signal from Linus.  

*"Exciting times," as Overstreet remarked—though perhaps not the kind users were hoping for.*
