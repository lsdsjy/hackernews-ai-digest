### Is life a form of computation?

#### [Submission URL](https://thereader.mitpress.mit.edu/is-life-a-form-of-computation/) | 195 points | by [redeemed](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=redeemed) | [135 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45352533)

# **Is Life a Form of Computation?**

### **Turing & Von Neumann’s Groundbreaking Insight**  

In 1994, a virtual machine on a computer screen performed something extraordinary—it copied and cloned itself, just as **John von Neumann** had predicted decades earlier. This revelation wasn't just a clever programming trick—it suggested something profound: **life itself might be computational at its core.**  

Alan Turing and von Neumann saw this connection early. DNA doesn’t just *resemble* a computer program—**it *is* one.** Every cell in your body reads genetic instructions like a machine executing code: *"If the codon is CGA, add an arginine to the protein."*  

### **How Biological and Digital Computing Differ**  

But biology isn’t just a clean, orderly Silicon Valley data center. The **key differences?**  
- **Massive Parallelism:** Your cells contain ~300 quintillion ribosomes—each like a microscopic, fault-tolerant computer.  
- **Noise & Randomness:** Biological reactions are probabilistic, jostled by thermal fluctuations. Unlike rigid digital logic gates, life embraces unpredictability.  
- **Decentralization:** DNA is just one layer—bacteria, viruses, and epigenetic effects all contribute to the "operating system" of life.  

### **Why Randomness is a Feature, Not a Bug**  

Turing insisted early computers include a **random number generator**—a crucial insight. Modern AI relies on randomness too:  
- **Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)** trains neural networks by adding controlled noise.  
- **Chatbots** use "temperature" settings to make replies less robotic.  
- **GPUs** accelerate AI through massive parallel processing (much like cells).  

### **Beyond the Von Neumann Architecture**  

Early computers were **centralized and sequential**, a product of 1940s tech limitations. But Turing and von Neumann imagined **alternatives:**  
- **Turing's "morphogenesis"** modeled biological patterns (like leopard spots) as emergent results of local chemical rules.  
- **Von Neumann’s "cellular automata"** showed how simple pixel-like units could self-replicate, foreshadowing DNA’s logic.  

### **The Future: Parallel, Distributed, Biological Computing**  

Today, AI is moving toward **biological-inspired architectures**—networks that aren’t just **efficient**, but **resilient, adaptive, and decentralized.**  

**The takeaway?** Life *is* computation—but of a wildly more complex, parallel, and chaotic kind than we’ve yet mastered.

### Always Invite Anna

#### [Submission URL](https://sharif.io/anna-alexei) | 1034 points | by [walterbell](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=walterbell) | [164 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45348495)

### The Power of Persistence: How One Friend’s Small Gesture Made All the Difference  

In a touching reflection on friendship and inclusion, Sharif Shameem shares a story about Anna, a shy, studious college peer who always declined invitations to parties—until one friend, Alexei, made sure she never stopped receiving them.  

#### The Invitations That Didn’t Need a "Yes"  
During their first semester, Shameem’s friend group bonded over Friday night parties—except for Anna, who consistently turned them down to study. Eventually, most of the group stopped asking her … but Alexei never did. Despite her predictable refusals, he made it a habit to invite her every time.  

When Shameem asked why, Alexei explained: *"I know she’s always going to say no, but that’s not the point. I invite her out so she’ll always feel included."*  

#### A Lifelong Impact  
Years later, Anna confided in Shameem that those small, persistent invitations had meant the world to her. Though she rarely joined, knowing she was still considered part of the group gave her comfort during a lonely transition to college life.  

#### A Lesson in Kindness  
The story serves as a quiet reminder: Sometimes, inclusion isn’t about the acceptance of an invitation—it’s about continuing to offer one.
