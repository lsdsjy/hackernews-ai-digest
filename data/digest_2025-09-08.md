### **Durable Queues: The Solution to Unbreakable AI & Workflow Systems**  

For years, **Jeremy Edberg**, former Reddit infrastructure lead, wrestled with **RabbitMQ** failures—delays, lost votes, and crashes when queues broke. Now, he reveals how **durable queues** fix these problems by combining **task queues with persistent database workflows**.  

#### 🔧 **Why Did Old Queues Fail?**  
At Reddit, task queues powered voting, comments, and submissions—but crashes meant **data loss**. Challenges included:  
- **No recovery** if a worker failed mid-task  
- **No way to retry** if a dependency (like a database) was down  
- **No built-in observability**—debugging was painful  

#### 🚀 **Enter Durable Queues**  
- **Checkpoints tasks** in a database (like Postgres), enabling **resumption from the last step** if a workflow fails.  
- **Persists workflows** (unlike Redis/Celery), so no more losing critical tasks.  
- **SQL-powered observability**—query queue status or past tasks easily.  

#### ⚖️ **Tradeoffs?**  
- **Higher durability** (great for critical workflows) but **lower throughput** than in-memory brokers like Redis.  
- Best for **big, important tasks** (transactional ops, AI workflows), not for **high-volume microtasks**.

### **The Data Illusion: How Identical Numbers Can Hide Wildly Different Stories**  

If you only looked at the summary statistics of Anscombe’s Quartet, you’d think the four datasets were nearly identical. Same means, same variances, same correlation coefficients, even the same regression lines. But when plotted, they reveal strikingly different patterns—everything from perfect linearity to wild outliers to a perfect curve.  

#### **The Four Deceptive Datasets**  

1. **The Straightforward Linear Model** – The first dataset looks exactly as you’d expect: neat, linear, and well-behaved.  
2. **The Hidden Curve** – The second dataset forms a perfect parabolic arc. Linear regression? Useless here.  
3. **The Rogue Outlier** – In the third dataset, a single outlier drags the regression line away from a perfect fit.  
4. **The Lone Dictator** – The fourth dataset shows a vertical line of points at x=8, with one distant point at x=19, creating an artificial correlation where none should exist.  

#### **Why This Matters**  

Created in 1973 by statistician **Francis Anscombe**, the quartet was designed to mock statisticians who relied solely on summary metrics. His point? **Graph your data before trusting numbers.**  

Modern variants like the **Datasaurus Dozen** (where points form a dinosaur while maintaining the same statistics) push the message further—never assume your data tells a simple story without looking.  

#### **Key Takeaways**  
- **Always visualize first.** Hidden patterns can lie beneath identical-looking stats.  
- **Check for outliers.** One anomaly can distort your analysis.  
- **Beware of spurious correlations.** The relationship might not be what it seems.  

Anscombe’s Quartet remains a classic lesson in **data skepticism**—an ever-relevant warning for anyone working with numbers today.

### **The Science: How Light Bends**  
Refraction happens when light changes speed moving between materials (like from air into glass). **Snell’s Law** governs this:  

$$n_1 \sin(\theta_1) = n_2 \sin(\theta_2)$$  

- **Key insight**: Light bends *toward* the normal (perpendicular line) when entering a denser material (like glass).  
- **Total internal reflection**: At extreme angles, light *bounces back* instead of passing through (think fiber optics).  

**→ Try it yourself**: Adjust the refractive indices in the interactive diagram below the article to see how light bends!  

---

### **Simplifying for the Browser**  
To keep things manageable, we assume:  
- **No complex 3D shapes**—just 2D circles (stretchable into rounded rectangles).  
- **Single refraction event**—light enters the glass but ignores exiting.  
- **Fixed background**—rays hit orthogonally (no perspective distortion).  

Not *perfectly* accurate—but close enough for a sleek visual effect.  

---

### **Crafting the Glass Surface**  
The "thickness" of our virtual glass is defined by a **height function**:  

```javascript  
const height = f(distanceFromEdge); // 0 (edge) → 1 (center)  
```  

The **derivative** of this function gives the *surface normal*, which determines how light bends.
