### Writing a C compiler in 500 lines of Python (2023)

#### [Submission URL](https://vgel.me/posts/c500/) | 235 points | by [ofou](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=ofou) | [76 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45117668)

# **Building a Tiny C Compiler in Just 500 Lines of Python**  

Writing a full-fledged C compiler is no small feat—especially when you limit yourself to **just 500 lines of Python**. Yet, that’s exactly what one developer set out to do, and the result is a surprisingly functional (if minimalist) compiler.  

### **Key Design Decisions**  
1. **Single-Pass Compilation**  
   - Most compilers first parse the input into an abstract syntax tree (AST) before generating code.  
   - To save space, this compiler emits WebAssembly code *as it parses*, skipping the AST entirely.  
   - Example: When it sees the **`~`** operator, it spits out WASM instructions immediately.  

2. **WebAssembly as the Target (Why?!)**  
   - Instead of targeting x86 or ARM, this compiler generates **WebAssembly**—even though WASM makes certain aspects harder.  
   - Challenges include:  
     - No `goto` (replaced with structured control flow blocks).  
     - Stack-based architecture *that still requires managing an extra stack* (because WASM doesn’t allow taking references to its stack).  

3. **Brutally Minimal Error Handling**  
   - There's no friendly, detailed error reporting—just a **`die()`** function that dumps a stack trace and (if you're lucky) a line number.  

### **What’s Missing?**  
To fit in 500 lines, the compiler **drops many C features**, including:  
- Preprocessor directives (`#define`, `#include`, etc.)  
- Structs and unions  
- Floating-point operations  
- Some control flow optimizations  

Despite these limitations, it still handles basic C constructs like loops, conditionals, functions, and pointers—making it a fascinating **educational project** for those curious about compilers.

# **What It's Like to Be a Bat—And Why We Can Never Really Know**  

Thomas Nagel’s 1974 paper *“What Is It Like to Be a Bat?”* isn’t just a philosophical musing—it’s a direct challenge to the way we understand consciousness itself. The core problem Nagel presents is simple yet profound: **No matter how much we empathize or imagine, we can never truly experience what it’s like to be another creature.**  

### **Why Bats?**  
Nagel chose bats because they possess a radically different way of perceiving the world—echolocation. We might *imagine* flying in the dark, navigating by sound waves, but Nagel argues that imagination isn’t the same as *experience*. Even if we gradually transformed into bats (body and all), our human-formed subjective experience would still color our understanding.  

### **The Hard Problem of Subjectivity**  
Nagel’s key insight: **Consciousness is inseparable from a single, subjective viewpoint.** This makes it **impossible to reduce experience to mere physical processes** (like brain states) without losing the essence of what it means to *feel* or *perceive* something.  

- **Reductive materialism fails**: If you strip away subjective experience ("what it’s like to be a bat"), you’re not explaining consciousness—you’re ignoring its defining feature.  
- **Objectivity has limits**: A purely scientific, third-person view can describe neural activity but can’t capture the *first-person sensation* of being something else.  

### **Critics & Counterarguments**  
Daniel Dennett, a staunch physicalist, disagrees—he argues that subjectivity isn’t as mysterious as Nagel claims. For Dennett, consciousness can still be explained through physical processes, even if it feels elusive.  

But Nagel’s point lingers: **Can science ever fully explain experience, or is there always an unbridgeable gap?**  

### **Why This Still Matters Today**  
The bat thought experiment remains influential in:  
✔ AI debates (*Can machines truly "experience" consciousness?*)  
✔ Animal rights (*Do animals have subjective experiences we fail to grasp?*)  
✔ Neuroscience (*Can brain scans ever capture the full depth of what it "feels like" to think?*)  

Nagel doesn’t claim to have answers—he just insists we can’t ignore the problem. **Consciousness isn’t just a puzzle to solve; it’s the very lens through which we see all puzzles.**  

So next time you see a bat darting through the night, ask yourself: **What’s really going on in that tiny, alien mind?** We might never know—but Nagel’s paper ensures we never stop wondering.
