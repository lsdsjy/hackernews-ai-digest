### Svelte’s characteristics that likely contribute most to improved performance

#### [Submission URL](https://chuniversiteit.nl/papers/svelte-is-fast) | 186 points | by [SlackingOff123](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=SlackingOff123) | [194 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45519915)

## **JavaScript Frameworks Showdown: The 2021 Study You Shouldn’t Ignore**  

Every developer faces the dilemma: *Which JavaScript framework should I use?* Most online benchmarks are oversimplified, unrealistic, or downright misleading. So, what if we turn to *actual academic research* for answers?  

A 2022 study published in the *Journal of Web Engineering* compared five major frameworks: **Angular, React, Vue, Svelte, and Blazor**. Despite being slightly dated (2021’s tech is ancient history, right?), its insights remain surprisingly relevant.  

### **Key Findings**  

#### **Rendering Strategies: Virtual DOM vs. Data Bindings**  
Frameworks handle UI updates in two major ways:  
- **Virtual DOM (vDOM)**: Used by **React, Vue, and Blazor**. These frameworks compare a "virtual" version of the DOM with the real one and apply only necessary changes. **Pro:** Efficient for large, dynamic apps. **Con:** Adds overhead by calculating diffs.  
- **Data Bindings**: Used by **Angular and Svelte**. Changes in state directly trigger DOM updates without a separate diffing step. **Pro:** More performant for fine-grained updates. **Con:** May require careful state management.  

**Who wins?** It depends. For **highly dynamic apps**, React’s vDOM might help. For **optimized rendering**, Svelte and Angular’s direct binding can be faster.  

#### **Creation vs. Updates: The Real Performance Differentiator**  
- **Creating elements?** All frameworks perform similarly—no big winner here.  
- **Updating elements?** Here’s where things get interesting:  
  - **Angular**: Walks the **entire component tree** every time, often doing unnecessary work.  
  - **React/Blazor**: Only re-renders **affected subtrees**—more efficient than Angular.  
  - **Vue/Svelte**: Only updates **"dirty" components** (parts of the UI that changed).  
    - **Vue** tracks changes *at runtime*.  
    - **Svelte** does this *at compile time*, potentially being the most optimized.  

### **Blazor’s Hidden Cost: WebAssembly Overhead**  
Unlike the others, **Blazor runs on WebAssembly (WASM)** instead of JavaScript, but there’s a catch—it **can’t access the DOM directly**. Instead, it relies on a **JS interop layer**, meaning extra overhead that can hurt performance.  

### **The Verdict (According to the Study)**  
- **Svelte** and **Vue** showed **better rendering efficiency** due to smart change detection.  
- **React’s vDOM** is solid for large apps but can lose to binding-based approaches in pure rendering speed.  
- **Angular’s full-tree updates** make it the slowest for fine-grained changes.  
- **Blazor’s WASM trade-off**: Potentially slower due to DOM access quirks, but appealing for C# devs.  

### **Final Thoughts**  
While the study is a few years old, the **fundamental trade-offs** (vDOM vs. bindings, WASM limitations) still matter today. **Svelte remains a speed demon**, React keeps scaling well, and Angular… well, Angular is Angular.  

**If you’re optimizing for raw rendering speed**, Svelte or Vue might be the way to go. **If ecosystem and stability matter more**, React is still king. **And if you love C#?** Blazor is intriguing—just know the WASM tax.  

**Check out the full paper**—even if the journal's `/index.php/` URL makes you question their web engineering chops. 😉

### We found a bug in Go's ARM64 compiler

#### [Submission URL](https://blog.cloudflare.com/how-we-found-a-bug-in-gos-arm64-compiler/) | 801 points | by [jgrahamc](https://news.ycombinator.com/user?id=jgrahamc) | [130 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45516000)

# **The Hidden Race Condition in Go's ARM64 Compiler That Broke Cloudflare's Stack Unwinding**  

### **A Needle in a 84-Million-Requests-Per-Second Haystack**  

Cloudflare processes **84 million HTTP requests per second**—which means even the tiniest bug can wreak havoc. Recently, their team stumbled upon a rare and **elusive bug in Go’s ARM64 compiler**, leading to **stack corruption and mysterious crashes** in their infrastructure.  

### **The First Clue: Mysterious Fatal Panics**  

Cloudflare’s monitoring first noticed **sporadic crashes** on ARM64 machines with errors like:  

> `fatal error: traceback did not unwind completely`  

This usually suggests **stack corruption**, but the bug only appeared in idle control-plane services—so they initially dismissed it.  

Then, **it kept happening.**  

### **The False Lead: Panic/Recover Issues**  

The team noticed a correlation between these crashes and **recovered panics** in Go's runtime. Digging deeper, they found a related Go issue ([#73259](https://github.com/golang/go/issues/73259)), suggesting a possible ARM64 **stack unwinding bug**.  

They "fixed" it by **removing panic/recover** for error handling, and the crashes **seemed to stop**...  

**But weeks later, the problem returned—worse than ever.**  

### **30 Crashes a Day, No Explanation**  

The fatal panics came back **without any panics to recover**, leaving Cloudflare with **30 crashes daily**—absolutely **no pattern, no correlation**:  

- Not tied to specific releases  
- Not linked to infrastructure changes  
- Not even the *position of Mars* could explain it  

Time to **dig deeper.**  

### **The Crash Patterns**  

Two main failure modes emerged:  

1. **Invalid Memory Access:** A goroutine accessing garbage memory causes a `SIGSEGV`.  
2. **Unwinding Disaster:** The stack unwinder hits corrupted data and fails with `traceback did not unwind completely`.  

All crashes traced back to **Go’s stack unwinding** (`(*unwinder).next`), particularly **during garbage collection (GC)**.  

### **The ARM64 Compiler Bug Revealed**  

Ultimately, Cloudflare discovered that Go’s **ARM64 compiler was emitting incorrect unwind directives**—**misaligning stack frame offsets** and causing race conditions.  

- This meant the **runtime couldn’t correctly traverse the stack**, leading to corruption.  
- The issue was **particularly nasty** because it **only manifested under rare conditions**—but Cloudflare’s scale made it inevitable.  

### **Fixing the Bug**  

After pinpointing the issue, the **Go team patched the ARM64 compiler**, ensuring proper stack frame alignment. Now, **Cloudflare’s stack unwinds correctly**—no more mysterious crashes.  

### **The Takeaway**  

- **Even tiny compiler bugs can have catastrophic effects at scale.**  
- **ARM64 edge cases are tricky—silent corruption isn’t just theoretical.**  
- **Never ignore sporadic crashes—they’re often signs of deep, lurking issues.**  

This was a **fascinating deep dive into how Go’s internals work**—and a great reminder that at scale, even the **rarest bugs become urgent problems.**
